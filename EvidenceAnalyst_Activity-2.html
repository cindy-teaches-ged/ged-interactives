Phase 1 — Review the Concepts
What Did the Video Teach Us?
Before we practice, let's get our tools ready. The video covered how to read passages for the GED Extended Response and decide which author makes the better argument.
Key Question to Ask Yourself as You Read:
"What does the writer want me to do or believe about this topic?" — That's the author's position.
Then ask: "Why should I believe them?" — That's the evidence.
🟢 Strong Evidence
  • Specific examples with details
  • Expert opinions (named experts)
  • Facts or statistics
  • Results from polls or surveys
  • Results from experiments or studies
  • Precise names, dates, research results
  • Recent sources
🟡 Weak Evidence
  • Unclear references ("some say…")
  • Unnamed experts
  • Outdated studies
  • Irrelevant details
  • Flawed reasoning / logical errors
  • Personal opinions with no facts
  • Vague generalizations

Phase 3 — Close Reading
Read & Mark the Passages
Below are two short passages on whether cities should ban plastic bags. Read both. Strong evidence is already highlighted in green and weak evidence in yellow. Study the highlights — then answer the discussion questions at the bottom.
Passage A — In Support of a Plastic Bag Ban

Plastic bags are one of the most damaging forms of waste on the planet. A 2023 report by the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) found that over 8 million metric tons of plastic enter the world's oceans each year, with single-use bags among the top five items found in coastal cleanups. Cities that have already adopted bans have seen measurable improvements. San Francisco, which enacted a plastic bag ban in 2007, reported a 72% reduction in plastic bag litter within two years, according to a study published in the journal Environmental Science & Policy.

Everyone knows that plastic bags are terrible for the environment — you can see them blowing around anywhere you go. Banning them is simply the right thing to do. Some environmentalists believe the ban will also reduce overall plastic consumption, though the exact effects are unclear. Cities that care about their residents should act immediately.

Passage B — Against a Mandatory Plastic Bag Ban

While reducing plastic waste is an important goal, mandatory bag bans are not the most effective solution. A 2021 study by the American Economic Review followed 400 grocery stores across five states and found that plastic bag bans led shoppers to purchase more thick plastic trash bags — increasing total plastic use by 12%. Small businesses bear a disproportionate cost from these bans. The National Federation of Independent Business surveyed 1,200 small retailers in 2022 and found that compliance costs averaged $4,800 per store in the first year.

Bag bans are really just a feel-good policy that doesn't solve the bigger problem. Many shoppers dislike the inconvenience of bringing reusable bags. A better approach might be education, though nobody has really tried this seriously yet. The government should focus on bigger issues before worrying about grocery bags.

Class Discussion — Talk through these together
