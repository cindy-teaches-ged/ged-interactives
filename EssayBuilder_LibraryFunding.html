Should Public Libraries Shift Funding Toward Digital Resources?
Passage C — In Favor of Shifting to Digital

Public libraries were designed to provide communities with free access to information. For most of the 20th century, that meant shelves of printed books, periodicals, and reference materials. But information no longer lives primarily on paper. Today, more than 85 percent of Americans have access to the internet at home, and digital content — e-books, audiobooks, online databases, and streaming media — has become the dominant way people consume information. Libraries that fail to adapt to this shift risk becoming irrelevant.

The data supports this transition. A 2023 report from the American Library Association found that e-book and audiobook checkouts increased by 33 percent over the previous five years, while physical book circulation declined by 14 percent in the same period. Libraries in cities like San Antonio and Columbus that invested heavily in digital platforms saw total patron engagement increase by over 20 percent, even as in-person visits leveled off. These numbers suggest that patrons are not abandoning libraries — they are using them differently.

Digital resources also address equity concerns more effectively than physical collections. A printed book can serve one reader at a time. A digital resource can serve dozens simultaneously. For patrons with disabilities, digital formats offer text-to-speech, adjustable font sizes, and screen reader compatibility — features that print books cannot provide. For working parents and shift workers who cannot visit a branch during operating hours, digital access means the library is available 24 hours a day.

Critics argue that shifting funds away from physical books will harm communities that depend on in-person library services. This concern deserves acknowledgment, but it does not require libraries to freeze their budgets in the past. A responsible transition does not eliminate physical resources — it reallocates funds to reflect how patrons are actually using the library. Every dollar spent maintaining a collection that fewer people use is a dollar not spent reaching the patrons who have already moved online.

Passage D — Against Shifting to Digital

The movement to redirect public library funding toward digital resources rests on an assumption that sounds modern but is dangerously incomplete: that because digital content is growing in popularity, it should receive a growing share of public investment. This logic ignores who libraries serve, how they serve them, and what would be lost if physical collections and in-person services were deprioritized.

Public libraries are not simply content delivery systems. They are physical spaces where communities gather, where children attend story hours, where adults take literacy classes, and where people experiencing homelessness access the only climate-controlled, safe public space available to them. A 2022 study by the Institute of Museum and Library Services found that libraries in low-income neighborhoods reported 40 percent more in-person visits per capita than libraries in higher-income areas. These patrons are not choosing between print and digital — they depend on the physical library because they have no alternative.

The digital divide remains a significant barrier. While proponents of digital funding cite high national internet access rates, those averages obscure deep disparities. The Federal Communications Commission reported that approximately 24 million Americans — disproportionately in rural areas, tribal communities, and low-income urban neighborhoods — still lack reliable broadband access. Shifting library funds toward digital resources effectively redirects public money away from the people who need public libraries the most and toward patrons who already have other options.

There is also a question of cost sustainability. E-book licensing agreements require libraries to repurchase access after a set number of checkouts — typically 26 loans or two years, whichever comes first. A single printed book, by contrast, can circulate for decades at no additional cost beyond occasional repair. The Panorama Project, a library industry research initiative, found that libraries spend an average of 45 percent more per title on digital content than on physical copies over a ten-year period. Framing digital investment as cost-effective ignores these long-term realities.

Libraries should absolutely offer digital resources. But redirecting core funding away from physical services and collections would disproportionately harm the most vulnerable patrons — the very people public libraries were created to serve.
